body_ja: |
  一九四〇年（昭和十五年）二月十一日。宇都宮の地で、ひとつの書道誌が産声をあげました。創刊したのは、臥龍会 初代会長・日賀野東華、三十六歳。以来八十五年、臥龍会は墨とともに歩んできました。これは、一つの家族と、それを支えた多くの仲間の物語です。

  ## 逓信省の役人が興した、月刊誌「臥龍」

  東華は当時、逓信省の官吏（宇都宮郵便局）でした。公務のかたわら、志を同じくする仲間とともに月刊競書誌「臥龍」を創刊します。全国を見渡しても、三十代半ばでこれほどの書道誌を興した例は、ほとんどなかったといいます。

  誌名の「臥龍」は、まだ天に昇らぬ、伏せた龍のこと。力を蓄え、時を待つその姿に、書に向かう心が重ねられています。

  ## 墨を支えた人、東華夫人・澄

  創刊を陰で支えたのは、東華の妻・澄でした。公務員は二つの職を持てないため、誌の編集発行人には澄が名を連ねます。以来、長きにわたり誌面を支え続けました。記念映像で「臥龍会の精神的支柱」と紹介された、静かな、しかし確かな存在でした。

  ## 戦火を越えて、一号も絶やさず

  創刊は、戦争のただ中。紙の確保もままならず、その厚さひとつにも苦心する日々でした。それでも「臥龍」は、創刊号から一度も途切れることなく発行を続けます。休みなく刊行を重ねてきたことは、全国でも稀な歩みです。通巻は、いまや千八百号を超えました。

  ## 戦後の復活、そして全国へ

  終戦後、「道」と名のつくものは一時すべて遠ざけられました。柔道も、剣道も、そして書道も。数年の時を経て書道が息を吹き返す兆しを見て、東華は子どもたちのための手本を整え、学校の課外事業を通じて「臥龍」は各地へ広がっていきます。とりわけ東北地方には、多くの支部が生まれました。逓信省で結ばれた各地の縁が、その礎になったといわれます。

  十年ごとの「臥龍書展」、子どもたちが五人一組で席上揮毫を競った「席書大会」。地域に根ざした行事が、書の裾野を広げてきました。

  ## 「習字」から「書道芸術」へ

  八十五年の対話のなかで、いまの会が見据えているのは未来です。文字を手で書く機会が減り、パソコンやタブレットが当たり前になった時代。伝えるべきは、技術だけではありません。文字がどこから来たのか、どんな成り立ちを持つのか。その理解ごと、次の世代へ渡していくこと。「習字」を越えて、「書道芸術」へ。そして、人生の後半を楽しむ方々へ、漢字文化圏の外から関心を寄せてくれる海外の人々へ。対象も、届け方も、時代とともに形を変えていきます。

  ## 三代、墨の家

  初代・日賀野東華から、二代・日賀野溥（現会長）、そして三代・日賀野琢へ。八十五年を数えた臥龍会は、これからも地道に、こつこつと、墨とともに歩んでいきます。
body_en: |
  On 11 February 1940, in the city of Utsunomiya, a calligraphy journal was born. Its founder was Tōka Higano, the first head of Garyukai, then thirty-six years old. For eighty-five years since, Garyukai has walked alongside ink. This is the story of one family, and of the many who stood beside it.

  ## An official of the Ministry of Communications, and the monthly Garyu

  At the time, Tōka was an official of the Ministry of Communications, working at the Utsunomiya post office. Alongside his public duties, together with like-minded companions, he launched the monthly calligraphy journal Garyu. Across the whole country, it is said, there were almost no cases of someone founding a journal of such standing in their mid-thirties.

  The name Garyu means a reclining dragon, one that has not yet risen into the sky. In that figure, gathering its strength and biding its time, is mirrored the spirit of facing the brush.

  ## The one who carried the ink: Sumi, Tōka's wife

  Behind the founding stood Tōka's wife, Sumi. Because a public servant could not hold a second post, it was Sumi whose name appeared as the journal's editor and publisher. For many years she sustained its pages. In an anniversary film she was introduced as "the spiritual pillar of Garyukai" — a quiet, yet certain, presence.

  ## Through the fires of war, never missing an issue

  The journal was founded in the midst of war. Paper was hard to secure, and even its thickness was a daily struggle. And yet Garyu continued, without a single break, from its very first issue. To have kept publishing without pause is a rare history even nationwide; the journal has now passed its eighteen-hundredth issue.

  ## Postwar revival, and the spread across Japan

  After the war, anything bearing the name of a "way" (dō) was set aside for a time — judo, kendo, and calligraphy among them. Seeing the first signs of calligraphy's return a few years later, Tōka prepared copybooks for children, and through schools' extracurricular programmes Garyu spread from place to place. In the Tōhoku region especially, many chapters were born. The ties formed across the country through the Ministry of Communications, it is said, became their foundation.

  The Garyu exhibition every ten years, and the seki-sho contests in which children competed in teams of five writing on the spot — events rooted in their communities widened the reach of the brush.

  ## From penmanship toward calligraphy as art

  In the conversations marking eighty-five years, what the society looks toward is the future. In an age when fewer people write by hand and computers and tablets are the norm, what must be passed on is not technique alone. Where characters came from, how they were formed — that understanding, too, must be handed to the next generation. Beyond penmanship, toward calligraphy as art. And toward those enjoying the later chapters of life, and people overseas, beyond the kanji cultural sphere, who turn to it with interest. Its audience, and the ways it reaches them, change their shape with the times.

  ## Three generations, a house of ink

  From the founder Tōka Higano, to the second-generation head Hiroshi Higano, and to the third generation, Taku Higano. Having counted eighty-five years, Garyukai will go on — steadily, patiently, walking alongside ink.
