  *臥龍会 八十五年のあゆみ（一九四〇–二〇二四）。創立から現在までの主な歩み。*

  ## 一九四〇年、宇都宮に生まれた月刊誌「臥龍」

  東華は当時、逓信省の官吏として宇都宮郵便局に勤めていました。公務のかたわら、志を同じくする仲間とともに、月刊競書誌「臥龍」を創刊します。全国を見渡しても、三十代半ばでこれほどの書道誌を興した例は、ほとんどなかったといいます。

  誌名の「臥龍」は、まだ天に昇らぬ、伏せた龍のこと。力を蓄え、時を待つその姿に、書に向かう心が重ねられています。表紙の題字は時代ごとに書き手を替えながら、いまも誌面の「顔」として受け継がれています。

  ## 墨を支えた人、東華夫人・澄

  創刊を陰で支えたのは、東華の妻・澄でした。十九歳で嫁ぎ、東華とは二十ほども年が離れていたといいます。誌の編集発行人には、長く澄の名が記されました。差出の宛名書きから発送まで、家族総出で誌を送り出した時代もあったといいます。記念映像で「臥龍会の精神的支柱」と紹介された、静かで、しかし確かな存在でした。

  ## 栃木県書道連盟の誕生と、臥龍会

  戦後まもない一九四七年（昭和二十二年）、栃木県の書家たちが集い、栃木県書道連盟が結成されます。その結成に向けた発起人会が開かれたのが、ほかならぬ臥龍会本部でした。東華は連盟の初代副会長として名を連ね、県内の書道界が一つにまとまっていく礎づくりに加わります。

  一つの会の枠を越え、地域全体の書の発展を見据える。その姿勢は、やがて臥龍会そのものの広がりにもつながっていきました。書は個人の芸であると同時に、人と人、土地と土地を結ぶものでもある——東華は早くから、そう考えていたのかもしれません。

  ## 戦火を越えて、一号も絶やさず

  創刊は、戦争のただ中でした。紙の確保もままならず、その厚さひとつにも苦心する日々。物資も人手も乏しいなか、誌を出し続けることは、並大抵のことではありませんでした。それでも「臥龍」は、創刊号から一度も途切れることなく発行を続けます。戦中も、戦後の混乱期も、休まずに。これほど長く、一号も絶やさずに刊行を重ねてきた競書誌は、全国でも稀です。通巻は、いまや千八百号を超えました。

  ## 戦後の復活、そして全国へ

  終戦後、「道」と名のつくものは一時すべて遠ざけられました。柔道も、剣道も、そして書道も。数年の時を経て書道が息を吹き返す兆しを見て、東華はいち早く、子どもたちのための手本を整えます。小学一年、二年、三年——学年ごとに用意された手本は、学校の課外事業を通じて各地へ届けられ、「臥龍」は教室から教室へと広がっていきました。

  とりわけ東北地方には、多くの支部が生まれました。逓信省で結ばれた各地の縁が、その礎になったといわれます。青森から福島まで、雪国の町々にも墨の輪が広がっていったのです。こうして臥龍会は、いまでは全国に約百三十の支部を持ち、およそ二千名の会員が学ぶ、栃木県内でも有数の書道団体へと育ちました。

  十年ごとに開かれた大規模な「臥龍書展」。そして、子どもたちが五人一組となり、その場で一気に筆を振るって競い合った「席書大会」。地域に根ざした行事の一つひとつが、幾世代もの書き手を育て、書の裾野を広げてきました。

  ## 二代、そして三代へ

  一九六一年（昭和三十六年）、二代・日賀野溥が宇都宮に戻り、会を継ぎます。以後、誌の編集発行を担い、創立五十周年、六十周年、七十周年、八十周年と、節目のたびに大きな書展を重ねてきました。二〇二〇年には誌面を大きく刷新し、そして二〇二四年、臥龍会は創立八十五周年を迎えます。

  いま会を預かるのは、三代・日賀野琢。古代文字（篆書・隷書）を専門とし、一書家としての創作に加え、企業や海外へと、書の新たな場を開いています。初代から数えて、三代。墨の家の歩みは、途切れることなく続いています。

  ## 「習字」から「書道芸術」へ

  八十五年の対話のなかで、いまの会が見据えているのは未来です。文字を手で書く機会が減り、パソコンやタブレットが当たり前になった時代。伝えるべきは、技術だけではありません。文字がどこから来たのか、どんな成り立ちを持つのか。その理解ごと、次の世代へ渡していくこと。ただ整えて書く「習字」を越えて、一人ひとりの表現としての「書道芸術」へ。

  受け手も、変わっていきます。人生の後半を墨とともに楽しむ方々へ。漢字文化圏の外から、日本の文字に惹かれて訪れる海外の人々へ。届け方も、紙の誌面からデジタルへと、少しずつ形を変えています。

  変わるもの、変わらないもの。その両方を抱えながら、臥龍会は地道に、こつこつと、これからも墨とともに歩んでいきます。
body_en: |
  Garyukai (書道研究 臥龍会) is a Japanese calligraphy society based in Utsunomiya, Tochigi. Since its founding in 1940 it has published the monthly calligraphy journal Garyu without missing a single issue, and has spread its chapters across Japan. As a research society specialising in the ancient seal and clerical scripts, it has sustained the culture of calligraphy for eighty-five years. This is its story, traced from the founder.

  On 11 February 1940, in the city of Utsunomiya, a calligraphy journal was born. Its founder was Tōka Higano, the first head of Garyukai, then thirty-six years old. For eighty-five years since, Garyukai has walked alongside ink. This is the story of one family, and of the many who stood beside it.

  ## 1940: the monthly Garyu, born in Utsunomiya

  At the time, Tōka served as an official of the Ministry of Communications at the Utsunomiya post office. Alongside his public duties, together with like-minded companions, he launched the monthly calligraphy journal Garyu. Across the whole country, it is said, there were almost no cases of someone founding a journal of such standing in their mid-thirties.

  The name Garyu means a reclining dragon, one that has not yet risen into the sky. In that figure, gathering its strength and biding its time, is mirrored the spirit of facing the brush. The title lettering on the cover has passed through different hands across the eras, and remains to this day the face of the journal.

  ## The one who carried the ink: Sumi, Tōka's wife

  Behind the founding stood Tōka's wife, Sumi. She married at nineteen, some twenty years younger than Tōka. For many years it was her name that appeared as the journal's editor and publisher. There were times when the whole family sent the issues out together, from addressing the envelopes to posting them. In an anniversary film she was introduced as "the spiritual pillar of Garyukai" — a quiet, yet certain, presence.

  ## The Tochigi Prefectural Calligraphy Federation, and Garyukai

  In 1947, not long after the war, the calligraphers of Tochigi came together and founded the Tochigi Prefectural Calligraphy Federation. The preparatory meeting that led to its founding was held at none other than the Garyukai headquarters. Tōka served as the federation's first vice-president, taking part in laying the foundations that would draw the prefecture's calligraphy world together.

  To look beyond the bounds of a single society, toward the flourishing of the whole region — that outlook would in time feed the growth of Garyukai itself. Calligraphy is a personal art, and at the same time something that binds person to person, place to place. Perhaps Tōka understood this from early on.

  ## Through the fires of war, never missing an issue

  The journal was founded in the midst of war. Paper was hard to secure, and even its thickness was a daily struggle. With materials and hands alike in short supply, to keep a journal in print was no ordinary feat. And yet Garyu continued, without a single break, from its very first issue — through the war and through the turmoil that followed. Few competitive calligraphy monthlies anywhere have been published so long without missing an issue; it has now passed its eighteen-hundredth.

  ## Postwar revival, and the spread across Japan

  After the war, anything bearing the name of a "way" (dō) was set aside for a time — judo, kendo, and calligraphy among them. Seeing the first signs of calligraphy's return a few years later, Tōka moved quickly to prepare copybooks for children. Grade by grade — first, second, third year of primary school — those copybooks reached schools through their extracurricular programmes, and Garyu spread from classroom to classroom.

  In the Tōhoku region especially, many chapters were born. The ties formed across the country through the Ministry of Communications, it is said, became their foundation; from Aomori down to Fukushima, circles of ink spread through the towns of the snow country. In time Garyukai grew into one of the largest calligraphy societies in Tochigi, with some 130 chapters and around 2,000 members across Japan.

  The large Garyu exhibition, held every ten years. And the seki-sho contests, in which children formed teams of five and competed writing on the spot, all at once, brush in hand. Each of these community-rooted events raised generation after generation of writers and widened the reach of the brush.

  ## To the second generation, and the third

  In 1961, the second-generation head, Hiroshi Higano, returned to Utsunomiya and took over the society. He carried the journal's editing and publishing, and marked the 50th, 60th, 70th and 80th anniversaries each with a major exhibition. In 2020 the journal was substantially renewed, and in 2024 Garyukai reached its 85th year.

  The society is now led by the third generation, Taku Higano. A specialist in the ancient seal and clerical scripts, he pursues his own work as an artist while opening new settings for calligraphy, for companies and overseas. Counting from the founder, three generations: the path of this house of ink continues unbroken.

  ## From penmanship toward calligraphy as art

  In the conversations marking eighty-five years, what the society looks toward is the future. In an age when fewer people write by hand and computers and tablets are the norm, what must be passed on is not technique alone. Where characters came from, how they were formed — that understanding, too, must be handed to the next generation. Beyond mere orderly penmanship, toward calligraphy as each person's own expression.

  Those it reaches are changing, too: people enjoying the later chapters of life alongside ink; and people from beyond the kanji cultural sphere, drawn to the characters of Japan. The ways it reaches them are shifting as well, little by little, from the printed page toward the digital.

  What changes, and what does not — holding both, Garyukai will go on, steadily and patiently, walking alongside ink.
